\section{Random variables}
A random variable encodes a numerical feature of an experiment, allowing us to study
its distribution and moments rather than every detail of the outcome. For example,
the number of heads in two coin tosses maps both $HT$ and $TH$ to $1$.
Formally, $X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ is a measurable function: the mapping is fixed,
but the realized value $X(\omega)$ depends on the random outcome $\omega$.
This uncertain numerical value explains the name ``random variable.''

\section{Probability and estimation identities}
Write $p$ for probability mass functions and $f$ for densities. Density identities
hold almost everywhere, and conditional ratios require positive denominators.
Assume the densities and finite second moments used below exist.

\partlabel{(a) General Bayes' rule: four cases}
The joint mass/density can be factored in either order. With rows specifying the
types of $(X,Y)$, these factorizations give
\[
\begin{array}{c@{\qquad}l}
\text{discrete, discrete}
 &p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\dfrac{p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)}{p_X(x)},\\[9pt]
\text{discrete, continuous}
 &f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\dfrac{p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)}{p_X(x)},\\[9pt]
\text{continuous, discrete}
 &p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\dfrac{f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)}{f_X(x)},\\[9pt]
\text{continuous, continuous}
 &f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\dfrac{f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)}{f_X(x)}.
\end{array}
\]
For example, in the mixed discrete--continuous case, for every measurable $B$,
\[
 p_X(x)\int_B f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy
 =\Prob(X=x,Y\in B)
 =\int_B p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,dy.
\]
Equality of the integrands proves the second formula; exchanging $X,Y$ proves
the third. The first and fourth follow directly by factoring $p_{X,Y}$ and
$f_{X,Y}$, respectively.

\partlabel{(b) General law of total probability: four cases}
Marginalize the joint distribution over $Y$ (sum for discrete $Y$, integrate
for continuous $Y$), then substitute its conditional factorization:
\[
\begin{array}{c@{\qquad}l}
\text{discrete, discrete}
 &p_X(x)=\displaystyle\sum_y p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y),\\[7pt]
\text{discrete, continuous}
 &p_X(x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,dy,\\[7pt]
\text{continuous, discrete}
 &f_X(x)=\displaystyle\sum_y f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y),\\[7pt]
\text{continuous, continuous}
 &f_X(x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,dy.
\end{array}
\]
The first two formulas sum or integrate $\Prob(X=x,Y\in\cdot)$.
For the last two, apply the same argument to $\Prob(X\in A)$ for every measurable
$A$, interchange nonnegative integrals/sums by Tonelli's theorem, and identify
the density of $X$.

\partlabel{(c) Law of total variance}
Let $m(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$. The tower property gives $\E[m(X)]=\E[Y]$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \E[\Var(Y\mid X)]+\Var(m(X))
 &=\E[Y^2]-\E[m(X)^2]+\E[m(X)^2]-(\E[Y])^2\\
 &=\Var(Y).
\end{aligned}
\]

\partlabel{(d) Linear least-squares estimate}
For $\Var(X)>0$, centering yields
\[
 \E[(Y-a-bX)^2]
 =\Var(Y)-2b\Cov(X,Y)+b^2\Var(X)
   +(\E[Y]-a-b\E[X])^2.
\]
First choose $a=\E[Y]-b\E[X]$; the remaining strictly convex quadratic is
minimized at $b=\Cov(X,Y)/\Var(X)$. Hence
\[
 \boxed{L[Y\mid X]=\E[Y]+\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\Var(X)}(X-\E[X]).}
\]
If $\Var(X)=0$, every affine predictor is constant almost surely and the best
predicted value is $\E[Y]$.

\partlabel{(e) Orthogonality of the MMSE estimate and its converse}
For $m(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$ and any square-integrable $\phi(X)$,
\[
 \E[(Y-m(X))\phi(X)]
 =\E\!\left[\phi(X)\E[Y-m(X)\mid X]\right]=0.
\]
Conversely, if a square-integrable $g(X)$ satisfies the same condition for every
such $\phi$, subtraction gives $\E[(m(X)-g(X))\phi(X)]=0$.
Taking $\phi(X)=m(X)-g(X)$ yields $\E[(m(X)-g(X))^2]=0$, so
$g(X)=\E[Y\mid X]$ almost surely. In particular, orthogonality implies
\[
 \E[(Y-g(X))^2]=\E[(Y-m(X))^2]+\E[(m(X)-g(X))^2],
\]
which also proves the MMSE property.

\partlabel{(f) A two-sided variance bound}
Chebyshev's inequality and the bound by $1$ give, for $a>0$,
\[
 \Prob(|Z-\E[Z]|\ge a)
 \le\min\!\left\{1,\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}\right\}
 \le\frac{2\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2}.
\]
For the last inequality, if $a^2\le\sigma^2$, the right-hand side is at least $1$;
if $a^2>\sigma^2$, use $\sigma^2+a^2\le2a^2$. This includes $\sigma^2=0$.

\partlabel{(g) General Hoeffding inequality}
Set $V=\sum_{k=1}^n(b_k-a_k)^2$ and $\mu=\E[S_n]$.
By independence and Hoeffding's lemma (proved in Problem~9),
\[
 \E[e^{\lambda(S_n-\mu)}]
 =\prod_{k=1}^n\E[e^{\lambda(X_k-\E[X_k])}]
 \le e^{\lambda^2V/8}.
\]
For $V,t>0$, exponential Markov inequality followed by $\lambda=4t/V$ gives
\[
 \Prob(S_n-\mu\ge t)\le e^{-\lambda t+\lambda^2V/8}
 =e^{-2t^2/V}.
\]
Apply the same argument to $-X_k$ and use the union bound:
\[
 \boxed{\Prob(|S_n-\mu|\ge t)\le
 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{k=1}^n(b_k-a_k)^2}\right).}
\]
At $t=0$ the claim is trivial. If $V=0$, $S_n=\mu$ almost surely, so the tail
probability is $0$ for $t>0$ (and $1$ for $t=0$).

\section{Exponential conditional expectations}
Use the rate convention $f_{X_i}(x)=\lambda_i e^{-\lambda_i x}$ for $x>0$.
Memorylessness gives $\E[X_i\mid X_i>t]=t+1/\lambda_i$, hence
\[
 \boxed{\E[X_1\mid X_1>2026]=2026+\frac1{\lambda_1}.}
\]
For truncation below $t$, integration by parts gives
\[
 \E[X_1\mid X_1<t]
 =\frac{\int_0^t x\lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1x}\,dx}{1-e^{-\lambda_1t}}
 =\frac1{\lambda_1}-\frac{t}{e^{\lambda_1t}-1}.
\]
Thus the second answer is $\boxed{1/\lambda_1-2026/(e^{2026\lambda_1}-1)}$.
Independence preserves the separate conditional laws under the three threshold
events, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
 &\E[X_1+X_2+X_3\mid X_1>1997,\ X_2>2014,\ X_3>2026]\\
 &\hspace{2em}=\boxed{6037+\frac1{\lambda_1}+\frac1{\lambda_2}+\frac1{\lambda_3}}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\section{Bivariate normal construction}
\partlabel{(a) Joint normality}
For any $a,b\in\mathbb R$,
\[
 aX+bY=a\mu_X+b\mu_Y+(a\sigma_X+b\rho\sigma_Y)Z_1
       +b\sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z_2.
\]
This is a linear combination of independent normal variables, hence normal
(possibly constant). Thus $(X,Y)$ is jointly Gaussian.

\partlabel{(b) Correlation}
Independence of $Z_1,Z_2$ gives
\[
 \Var(X)=\sigma_X^2,\qquad \Var(Y)=\sigma_Y^2,\qquad
 \Cov(X,Y)=\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y,
 \qquad \boxed{\Corr(X,Y)=\rho}.
\]

\partlabel{(c) Joint density}
Put $u=(x-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $v=(y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$.
The inverse map is $z_1=u$, $z_2=(v-\rho u)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$, with
absolute Jacobian $1/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2})$. Transforming the
independent standard-normal density therefore gives, for all $x,y\in\mathbb R$,
\[
 \boxed{f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{u^2-2\rho uv+v^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right].}
\]

\section{A three-state Markov chain}
With state order $(1,2,3)$ and $p_{ij}=\Prob(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)$, the diagram gives
\[
 P=\begin{pmatrix}1/4&0&3/4\\1/2&0&1/2\\1/2&1/4&1/4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\partlabel{(a) One-step probabilities}
Time homogeneity gives
$\Prob(X_5=3\mid X_4=2)=p_{23}=\boxed{1/2}$ and
$\Prob(X_4=1\mid X_3=2)=p_{21}=\boxed{1/2}$.

\partlabel{(b) Stationary distribution}
Solving $\boldsymbol\pi P=\boldsymbol\pi$ gives
$\pi_2=\pi_3/4$ and $3\pi_1/4=\pi_2/2+\pi_3/2$, hence
$\pi_1=5\pi_3/6$. Normalization yields
\[
 \boxed{\boldsymbol\pi=(2/5,\,3/25,\,12/25).}
\]
\partlabel{(c) Periods}
The positive-probability returns $2\to3\to2$ and $2\to3\to3\to2$ have lengths
$2$ and $3$. Thus $d(2)=\gcd(2,3)=1$.
All states communicate, so the entire chain has period $1$.

\partlabel{(d) Path probability}
By the Markov property,
\[
 \Prob(X_0=3,X_1=2,X_2=1)=\frac15\,p_{32}p_{21}
 =\frac15\cdot\frac14\cdot\frac12=\boxed{\frac1{40}}.
\]
\partlabel{(e) Two-step conditional moments}
The first row of $P^2$ is $(7,3,6)/16$. Therefore
\[
 \E[X_5\mid X_3=1]=\frac{7+2\cdot3+3\cdot6}{16}=\boxed{\frac{31}{16}},
 \qquad
 \E[X_5^2\mid X_3=1]=\frac{7+4\cdot3+9\cdot6}{16}=\frac{73}{16},
\]
\[
 \boxed{\Var(X_5\mid X_3=1)=\frac{73}{16}-\left(\frac{31}{16}\right)^2
 =\frac{207}{256}.}
\]

\section{Estimating a coin's head probability}
Assume $n\ge1$ and $0\le k\le n$. The likelihood is
$L(p)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ on $[0,1]$.

\partlabel{(a) MLE}
For $0<k<n$, $\ell'(p)=k/p-(n-k)/(1-p)=0$ gives $p=k/n$;
$\ell''(p)<0$ verifies maximality. If $k=0$ or $n$, the maximum is at $0$ or $1$.
Thus $\boxed{\hat p_{\mathrm{MLE}}=k/n}$.

\partlabel{(b) MAP}
Since $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)=\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$,
\[
 f(p\mid X=k)\propto p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad
 p\mid X=k\sim\operatorname{Beta}(k+1,n-k+1).
\]
The uniform prior leaves the likelihood maximizer unchanged, so
$\boxed{\hat p_{\mathrm{MAP}}=k/n}$, including the endpoint modes on $[0,1]$.

\partlabel{(c) MMSE}
The posterior mean gives
$\boxed{\hat p_{\mathrm{MMSE}}=\E[p\mid X=k]=(k+1)/(n+2)}$.

\partlabel{(d) LLSE}
Under the prior-predictive joint distribution,
\[
 \E[p]=\frac12,\quad \Var(p)=\frac1{12},\quad
 \E[X]=\E[np]=\frac n2,\quad
 \Cov(X,p)=\Cov(\E[X\mid p],p)=\frac n{12}.
\]
The total-variance formula gives
\[
 \Var(X)=\E[np(1-p)]+\Var(np)=\frac n6+\frac{n^2}{12}
 =\frac{n(n+2)}{12}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 \boxed{\hat p_{\mathrm{LLSE}}=\frac12+
 \frac{n/12}{n(n+2)/12}\left(k-\frac n2\right)=\frac{k+1}{n+2}.}
\]

\section{Gaussian MMSE equals LLSE: two methods}
Let $\mu_X=\E[X]$, $\mu_Y=\E[Y]$, $\sigma_X^2=\Var(X)>0$, and
$\beta=\Cov(X,Y)/\sigma_X^2$.

\partlabel{Method 1: an independent Gaussian residual}
Define $Z=Y-\mu_Y-\beta(X-\mu_X)$. Then $\E[Z]=0$ and
\[
 \Cov(X,Z)=\Cov(X,Y)-\beta\Var(X)=0.
\]
Because $(X,Z)$ is jointly Gaussian, zero covariance implies independence.
Thus $\E[Z\mid X]=0$, and
\[
 \boxed{\E[Y\mid X]=\mu_Y+\beta(X-\mu_X)=L[Y\mid X].}
\]
The last equality also follows from Problem~2(d).

\partlabel{Method 2: completing the square in the conditional density}
For $\sigma_Y>0$ and $|\rho|<1$, standardize to
$U=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $V=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$.
Using $u^2-2\rho uv+v^2=(v-\rho u)^2+(1-\rho^2)u^2$,
\[
 f_{V\mid U}(v\mid u)=\frac{f_{U,V}(u,v)}{f_U(u)}
 =\frac{\exp[-(v-\rho u)^2/(2(1-\rho^2))]}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}}.
\]
Hence $\E[V\mid U=u]=\rho u$, and transforming back gives
\[
 \E[Y\mid X=x]=\mu_Y+\frac{\rho\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)
 =\mu_Y+\beta(x-\mu_X)=L[Y\mid X=x].
\]
For completeness, if $\sigma_Y=0$ then $Y=\mu_Y$ almost surely. If $|\rho|=1$,
$\Var(V-\rho U)=0$, so $V=\rho U$ almost surely and the same answer follows.
If $\Var(X)=0$, the ratio is undefined, but both estimates reduce to $\E[Y]$.

\section{Expected return of the uniform-sum program}
Let $U_i$ be independent $\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$ increments,
$S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nU_i$, and $N=\min\{n\ge1:S_n>1\}$.
The program returns $S_N$, not $N$. The volume of the simplex
$\{u_i\ge0:\sum_{i=1}^n u_i\le1\}$ is $1/n!$, so
\[
 \Prob(N>n)=\Prob(S_n\le1)=\frac1{n!},\qquad
 \E[N]=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\Prob(N>n)=e.
\]
The event $\{N\ge i\}=\{S_{i-1}\le1\}$ depends only on earlier increments and
is independent of $U_i$. By Tonelli's theorem,
\[
 \E[S_N]=\sum_{i=1}^\infty\E[U_i\mathbf1_{\{N\ge i\}}]
 =\frac12\sum_{i=1}^\infty\Prob(N\ge i)
 =\boxed{\frac e2\approx1.35914}.
\]

\section{Hoeffding's lemma}
Suppose $\E[X]=0$ and $a\le X\le b$. If $a=b$, then $X=0$ almost surely and
the claim is immediate. Otherwise $a\le0\le b$ and convexity gives
\[
 e^{\lambda x}\le\frac{b-x}{b-a}e^{\lambda a}
                  +\frac{x-a}{b-a}e^{\lambda b},\qquad a\le x\le b.
\]
Taking expectations and putting $p=-a/(b-a)\in[0,1]$ and $t=\lambda(b-a)>0$,
\[
 \E[e^{\lambda X}]\le(1-p)e^{-pt}+pe^{(1-p)t}=e^{h(t)},\qquad
 h(t)=-pt+\log(1-p+pe^t).
\]
We have $h(0)=h'(0)=0$ and
\[
 h''(t)=\frac{p(1-p)e^t}{(1-p+pe^t)^2}
       =q(t)(1-q(t))\le\frac14,
 \qquad q(t)=\frac{pe^t}{1-p+pe^t}\in[0,1].
\]
Integrating twice yields $h(t)=\int_0^t(t-s)h''(s)\,ds\le t^2/8$.
Therefore
\[
 \boxed{\E[e^{\lambda X}]\le\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2(b-a)^2}{8}\right).}
\]

\section{Utility, fairness, and reinforcement-learning rewards}
Throughout, $\mathcal F=\{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^2:x_1+2x_2\le12\}$;
logarithmic objectives require $x_1,x_2>0$.

\partlabel{(a) What a utility function represents}
A utility function represents deterministic preferences when
\[
 x\succeq y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u(x)\ge u(y).
\]
Any strictly increasing transformation preserves this ranking. Thus $x$ and
$\log x$ both mean ``more service is better,'' but their sums need not have the
same maximizer: on $\mathcal F$, they yield $(12,0)$ and $(6,3)$, respectively
(derived in part (c)). Individual rankings do not specify utility differences
or comparisons between users; utility scales and weights supply these extra
interpersonal trade-offs.

\partlabel{(b) Concavity, diminishing returns, and transfers}
The conditions $u'(x)>0$ and $u''(x)<0$ mean that service increases utility,
but its marginal benefit decreases. Figure~\ref{fig:utility} shows a representative
curve and the decreasing tangent slopes.

For $0<a<b$ and $0<\delta<(b-a)/2$, define
$H(\delta)=u(a+\delta)+u(b-\delta)$. Since $a+\delta<b-\delta$ and $u'$ is
strictly decreasing,
\[
 H'(\delta)=u'(a+\delta)-u'(b-\delta)>0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 u(a+\delta)+u(b-\delta)>u(a)+u(b).
\]
This transfer preserves total \emph{service}, not necessarily total
\emph{resource use}. For example, moving $\delta$ service from user 1 to user 2
increases $x_1+2x_2$ by $\delta$. At an interior optimum with equal weights,
$u'(x_1)=u'(x_2)/2$: marginal utility per unit of resource, rather than service,
is equalized. Thus strict concavity need not imply equal rates; log utility
gives $(6,3)$.

\partlabel{(c) Optimal allocations and proportional fairness}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Total service:} $x_1+x_2\le x_1+2x_2\le12$, with equality
 only at $(12,0)$.
 \item \textbf{Log utility:} monotonicity makes the constraint bind. For
 $f(x_2)=\log(12-2x_2)+\log x_2$, the equation
 $f'(x_2)=-2/(12-2x_2)+1/x_2=0$ gives $x_2=3$, hence $(6,3)$.
 Strict concavity ensures the unique maximum.
 \item \textbf{Minimum service:} if $t=\min\{x_1,x_2\}$, then
 $3t\le x_1+2x_2\le12$. The bound $t=4$ is attained at $(4,4)$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Objective & Allocation & Total service & Minimum service\\
\midrule
Total service & $(12,0)$ & $12$ & $0$\\
Log utility & $(6,3)$ & $9$ & $3$\\
Minimum service & $(4,4)$ & $8$ & $4$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Log utility implements \emph{proportional fairness}, neither maximum throughput
nor equal service. Indeed, for a maximizer $\mathbf x^*$ over a convex
$\mathcal C\subseteq\mathbb R_{>0}^n$, every segment
$\mathbf x^*+t(\mathbf y-\mathbf x^*)$, $0\le t\le1$, is feasible. Optimality gives
\[
 \boxed{\left.\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i\log(x_i^*+t(y_i-x_i^*))\right|_{t=0^+}
 =\sum_i\frac{y_i-x_i^*}{x_i^*}\le0.}
\]
Thus no feasible change has a positive sum of users' proportional service changes:
percentage gains cannot outweigh percentage losses relative to $\mathbf x^*$.

For $2\log x_1+\log x_2$, the binding constraint and first-order condition give
\[
 -\frac4{12-2x_2}+\frac1{x_2}=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{(x_1^*,x_2^*)=(8,2)}.
\]
The coefficient $2$ gives user 1 twice the utility weight, so the same proportional
increase counts twice as much. It implements \emph{weighted proportional fairness};
it does not require twice the service or literally represent two users.

\partlabel{(d) Concavity under uncertainty}
For the certain payoff A and lottery B,
\[
 \E[X_A]=\E[X_B]=5,\qquad
 \E[\log X_A]=\log5>\tfrac12\log1+\tfrac12\log9=\log3.
\]
Expected-payoff maximization is indifferent; expected-log-utility maximization
strictly prefers A. The certainty equivalent of B is
$\boxed{c=\exp(\E[\log X_B])=3}$.
Generally, Jensen's inequality gives $\E[u(X)]\le u(\E[X])$ for concave $u$:
the certain mean is weakly preferred to the lottery (strictly so for strictly
concave $u$ and a nonconstant lottery, when expectations exist).
This is \emph{risk aversion across outcomes for one user}; part (b) concerns
\emph{fairness across different users}.

\partlabel{(e) The von Neumann--Morgenstern theorem}
For lotteries $P,Q,R$ over a finite outcome set $\{z_1,\ldots,z_m\}$, the four
standard preference axioms are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
 \item \textbf{Completeness:} $P\succeq Q$ or $Q\succeq P$ for every pair.
 \item \textbf{Transitivity:} $P\succeq Q$ and $Q\succeq R$ imply $P\succeq R$.
 \item \textbf{Continuity:} if $P\succ Q\succ R$, some $\alpha\in(0,1)$ satisfies
 $Q\sim\alpha P+(1-\alpha)R$.
 \item \textbf{Independence:} for every $\alpha\in(0,1)$,
 $P\succeq Q$ if and only if
 $\alpha P+(1-\alpha)R\succeq\alpha Q+(1-\alpha)R$.
\end{enumerate}
These axioms are equivalent to an expected-utility representation:
\[
 P\succeq Q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \sum_j P(z_j)u(z_j)\ge\sum_j Q(z_j)u(z_j).
\]
The utility index is unique up to positive affine transformations
$v=au+b$, $a>0$. The theorem requires neither concavity nor logarithmic utility
and does not determine interpersonal fairness.
An arbitrary increasing transformation preserves deterministic rankings but may
change lottery preferences, because it need not commute with expectation:
part (d) has $A\sim B$ under $u(x)=x$ but $A\succ B$ under $v(x)=\log x$.

\partlabel{(f) From utility to episodic RL rewards}
The \emph{reward hypothesis} proposes that a goal can be expressed as maximizing
the expected cumulative sum of a scalar reward. In this undiscounted,
fixed-horizon setting, $J(\pi)=\E_\pi[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}R_{t+1}]$.
Like VNM, this represents preferences by an expectation of a scalar quantity.
However, VNM alone supplies neither temporal additivity nor a stationary Markov
reward on a prescribed state space.

The two fairness objectives are
\[
\begin{aligned}
 J_{\mathrm{instant}}(\pi)
 &=\E_\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\bigl(\log X_1(t)+\log X_2(t)\bigr)\right],\\
 J_{\mathrm{average}}(\pi)
 &=\E_\pi[\log\bar X_1+\log\bar X_2],
 \qquad \bar X_i=\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}X_i(t).
\end{aligned}
\]
For $T=2$, both schedules have average allocation $(6,3)$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Schedule & Allocations & $J_{\mathrm{instant}}$ & $J_{\mathrm{average}}$\\
\midrule
$\pi_A$ & $(6,3),\ (6,3)$ & $2\log18=\log324$ & $\log18$\\
$\pi_B$ & $(10,1),\ (2,5)$ & $2\log10=\log100$ & $\log18$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Thus the objectives are not equivalent: the first strictly prefers A, while the
second is indifferent. Even dividing $J_{\mathrm{instant}}$ by $T$ does not remove
this difference; concavity penalizes temporal fluctuations under instantaneous
utility.

Instantaneous utility uses $R_{t+1}=\log X_1(t)+\log X_2(t)$.
For average-service utility, let $C_i(t)=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}X_i(s)$, with $C_i(0)=0$,
and use the terminal reward
\[
 R_{t+1}=\begin{cases}
 0, & 0\le t<T-1,\\[2pt]
 \log\!\dfrac{C_1(t)+X_1(t)}{T}
 +\log\!\dfrac{C_2(t)+X_2(t)}{T}, & t=T-1.
 \end{cases}
\]
Pathwise, $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}R_{t+1}=\log\bar X_1+\log\bar X_2$, so its expectation
is exactly $J_{\mathrm{average}}$. Augment a Markov environment state to
$\widetilde S_t=(S_t,C_1(t),C_2(t),t)$, updating
$C_i(t+1)=C_i(t)+X_i(t)$. The accumulated service and clock make this a Markov
reward with no additional external time dependence; complete histories are unnecessary.

\partlabel{(g) VNM and the reward hypothesis: connection and limits}
Treat a complete trajectory $\tau$ as an outcome and a policy as inducing a lottery
over trajectories. When the relevant expected-utility representation exists,
preferences take the form $\E_\pi[U(\tau)]$; RL realizes this form when
$U(\tau)=\sum_tR_{t+1}$. For example, part (f)'s terminal reward realizes utility
of average service exactly.
VNM is a conditional representation theorem, not a proof that every goal admits
an additive stationary Markov reward on the original state space. Temporal
structure, sufficient state information, and fairness weights are additional
modeling choices. Moreover, applying the finite-outcome theorem to continuous
trajectory spaces requires appropriate additional assumptions.
